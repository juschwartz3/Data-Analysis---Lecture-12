# Literature Search: Neuropeptide Y Y1 Receptor (1995–2026)

---

**Franco-Cereceda, A.** et al. (1998). *Neuropeptide Y Y1 receptors in vascular pharmacology.* European Journal of Pharmacology. PMID: [9669490](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9669490/). First author: A. Franco-Cereceda. This review established that NPY Y1 receptors mediate the majority of direct peripheral vascular effects of neuropeptide Y. Released alongside noradrenaline from sympathetic nerve terminals, NPY causes non-adrenergic vasoconstriction that can be selectively blocked by non-peptide Y1 antagonists. The work was foundational in clarifying the role of Y1 receptors in sympathetic co-transmission and identifying them as pharmacologically tractable targets in cardiovascular disease.

---

**Kushi, A.** et al. (1998). *Obesity and mild hyperinsulinemia found in neuropeptide Y-Y1 receptor-deficient mice.* Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA, 95(26):15659–64. PMID: [9861026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9861026/). First author: A. Kushi. This landmark study generated Y1 receptor-null mice by gene targeting, expecting reduced food intake and leanness. Contrary to predictions, Y1-R−/− mice developed moderate obesity and mild hyperinsulinemia without hyperphagia. The obesity was attributed to impaired insulin secretion and reduced expression of uncoupling protein UCP2 in white adipose tissue. The paradoxical phenotype was interpreted as evidence of compensatory upregulation of other NPY receptor subtypes during development.

---

**Kask, A.** et al. (1998). *Anxiogenic-like effect of the NPY Y1 receptor antagonist BIBP3226 administered into the dorsal periaqueductal gray matter in rats.* Regulatory Peptides. PMID: [9802417](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9802417/). First author: A. Kask. Bilateral microinjection of the selective Y1 antagonist BIBP3226 into the dorsal periaqueductal gray (DPAG) produced anxiogenic-like behavior in the elevated plus-maze without altering locomotor activity. Injections into other brain regions—including the amygdala and hypothalamus—had no significant effect. These findings provided site-specific evidence that endogenous NPY tonically suppresses anxiety-related behavior in the DPAG through Y1 receptors.

---

**Caberlotto, L.** et al. (1999). *Alterations in neuropeptide Y levels and Y1 binding sites in the Flinders Sensitive Line rats, a genetic animal model of depression.* Neuroscience Letters. PMID: [10327163](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10327163/). First author: L. Caberlotto. In Flinders Sensitive Line (FSL) rats—a well-validated genetic model of depression—hippocampal NPY levels were reduced while Y1 receptor binding was elevated compared to controls. Fluoxetine treatment normalized these regional changes, with increased NPY expression in several brain areas and enhanced Y1 binding in the amygdala and occipital cortex. The results implicated Y1 receptor dysregulation, but not Y2, in depressive pathophysiology.

---

**Reubi, J.C.** et al. (2001). *Y(1)-mediated effect of neuropeptide Y in cancer: breast carcinomas as targets.* Cancer Research. PMID: [11389101](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11389101/). First author: J.C. Reubi. This study found NPY receptors in 85% of primary breast carcinomas and 100% of lymph node metastases, with Y1 predominating—in contrast to normal breast tissue, which expresses Y2. In Y1-expressing tumor cells, NPY produced a dose-dependent inhibition of cell growth exceeding 40%. The shift from Y2 to Y1 subtype upon neoplastic transformation was identified as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target, opening avenues for NPY-analogue-based tumor imaging and treatment.

---

**Ishida, H.** et al. (2007). *Infusion of neuropeptide Y into CA3 region of hippocampus produces antidepressant-like effect via Y1 receptor.* Hippocampus. PMID: [17265460](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17265460/). First author: Hisahito Ishida. Using the learned helplessness (LH) rat model of depression, researchers showed that NPY infused into the CA3 hippocampal subregion—but not the dentate gyrus—produced antidepressant-like effects. Co-administration of the Y1 antagonist BIBO3304 blocked this effect, while the Y2 antagonist BIIE0246 did not. These findings identified the CA3 Y1 receptor as a specific mediator of NPY's antidepressant action and highlighted a potential target for novel antidepressant therapies.

---

**Brothers, S.P.** and Bhatt, C. (2010). *Therapeutic potential of neuropeptide Y (NPY) receptor ligands.* EMBO Molecular Medicine, 2(11):429–439. PMC: [PMC3394504](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3394504/). First author: Shaun P. Brothers. This comprehensive review surveyed the therapeutic landscape of NPY receptor-targeting compounds, covering obesity, anxiety, depression, pain, bone loss, and cancer. Despite extensive preclinical evidence supporting Y1 and Y5 receptor antagonism for obesity and anxiolytic indications, no NPY-targeting drug had entered clinical approval at time of publication. The authors outlined the pharmacological challenges—poor bioavailability, CNS penetration, and receptor subtype selectivity—and urged continued development of selective small-molecule ligands.

**Lee, N.J.** et al. (2011). *Osteoblast specific Y1 receptor deletion enhances bone mass.* Bone. PMID: [21040809](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21040809/). First author: Nicola J. Lee. Conditional deletion of the Y1 receptor selectively in osteoblasts recapitulated the high-bone-mass phenotype of germline Y1-null mice. Femoral cancellous bone volume, trabecular thickness, and trabecular number were all markedly increased, alongside elevated mineral apposition rates and upregulated expression of osteocalcin, alkaline phosphatase, and dentin matrix protein-1. The study confirmed that Y1 receptor signaling within osteoblasts—not through neuronal or other systemic routes—is responsible for the inhibitory effect of NPY on bone formation.

---

**Morales-Medina, J.C.** et al. (2012). *Role of neuropeptide Y Y₁ and Y₂ receptors on behavioral despair in a rat model of depression with co-morbid anxiety.* Neuropharmacology. PMID: [21803058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21803058/). First author: Julio César Morales-Medina. Using olfactory bulbectomized (OBX) rats—a model exhibiting both depression and anxiety—researchers administered Y1 and Y2 receptor-selective agonists and antagonists via 2-week intracerebroventricular infusion. Activation of Y1 receptors with [Leu³¹Pro³⁴]PYY decreased depressive- and anxiogenic-like behaviors across the forced swim test, open field, and social interaction tests, while Y2 receptor activation worsened depression markers. Elevated Y2 receptor binding was found in hippocampus and basolateral amygdala of OBX rats, suggesting a bidirectional relationship between Y receptor subtypes in emotional disorders.

---

**Sousa, D.M.** et al. (2012). *Neuropeptide Y Y1 receptor antagonism increases bone mass in mice.* Bone. PMID: [22484690](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22484690/). First author: Daniela M. Sousa. Oral administration of the selective Y1 antagonist BIBO3304 for eight weeks dose-dependently increased cancellous bone volume by up to 1.5-fold, improved trabecular architecture, and elevated osteoblast mineral apposition rates in both cortical and cancellous compartments. These benefits occurred without adverse metabolic or endocrine effects. The study provided the first pharmacological proof-of-concept that Y1 receptor blockade can enhance bone mass in vivo, positioning Y1 antagonists as candidate anabolic therapeutics for osteoporosis.

**Yang, Z.** et al. (2018). *Structural basis of ligand binding modes at the neuropeptide Y Y1 receptor.* Nature, 556(7702):520–524. PMID: [29670288](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29670288/). DOI: [10.1038/s41586-018-0046-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0046-x). First author: Zhenlin Yang. This study reported crystal structures of the human Y1 receptor bound to two selective non-peptide antagonists (UR-MK299 and BMS-193885) at 2.7 Å and 3.0 Å resolution. Combined with mutagenesis, NMR, photo-crosslinking, and molecular docking, the work revealed the determinants of antagonist selectivity and—for the first time—mapped the interaction of the N-terminus of NPY with the receptor. The structures provide a framework for structure-based drug discovery targeting NPY receptors in obesity, bone disease, and cancer.

**Park, C.** and Kim, J. et al. (2022). *Structural basis of neuropeptide Y signaling through Y1 receptor.* Nature Communications, 13(1):853. PMID: [35165283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35165283/). DOI: [10.1038/s41467-022-28510-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-28510-6). First authors: Chaehee Park and Jinuk Kim (contributed equally). Using cryo-electron microscopy, this study resolved the structure of the NPY-bound Y1 receptor in complex with the Gi1 heterotrimer. The NPY C-terminal segment adopts an extended conformation that inserts deep into the transmembrane core, with the amidated C-terminal residue Y36 anchored at the base of the binding pocket. The helical mid-region and two N-terminal residues of NPY additionally contact extracellular loops, collectively explaining the high-affinity agonist recognition mechanism.

**Tang, T.** et al. (2022). *Receptor-specific recognition of NPY peptides revealed by structures of NPY receptors.* Science Advances, 8(18):eabm1232. PMID: [35507650](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35507650/). DOI: [10.1126/sciadv.abm1232](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abm1232). First author: Tingting Tang. Cryo-EM structures of human Y1, Y2, and Y4 receptors bound to NPY or pancreatic polypeptide in complex with Gi1 protein enabled a direct cross-subtype structural comparison. The C-terminus of NPY is conserved across subtypes, while differential engagement of the peptide N-terminus and extracellular loops determines subtype specificity. The conformational plasticity of NPY in each binding pose provides the molecular basis for receptor selectivity and a roadmap for developing subtype-selective therapeutics.

**Nelson, T.S.** et al. (2022). *Spinal neuropeptide Y Y1 receptor-expressing neurons are a pharmacotherapeutic target for the alleviation of neuropathic pain.* Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA. PMID: [36343228](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36343228/). DOI: [10.1073/pnas.2204515119](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2204515119). First author: Tyler S. Nelson. Chemogenetic and pharmacological approaches demonstrated that Y1 receptor-expressing interneurons (Y1-INs) in the spinal dorsal horn are both necessary and sufficient to produce neuropathic pain behaviors. Activation of Y1-INs in naïve mice induced mechanical and cold allodynia, while inhibition in injured mice reversed hypersensitivity. Intrathecal delivery of a Y1-selective agonist reduced pain by acting on dorsal horn neurons rather than peripheral afferents, establishing spinal Y1-INs as a tractable target for neuropathic pain treatment.

**Shen, S.** et al. (2024). *Structural basis of neuropeptide Y signaling through Y1 and Y2 receptors.* MedComm, 5(7):e565. PMID: [38882210](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38882210/). DOI: [10.1002/mco2.565](https://doi.org/10.1002/mco2.565). First author: Siyuan Shen. Three cryo-EM structures were reported: Gi2-coupled Y1R bound to NPY, Y1R bound to the selective agonist [Leu³¹,Pro³⁴]-NPY, and Y2R bound to NPY. Comparative analysis identified a conserved core peptide-binding mode alongside a divergent sub-pocket that confers subtype selectivity and has undergone adaptive evolution across species. Cell-based assays validated the structural observations, providing a detailed molecular atlas for guiding the rational design of selective Y1 and Y2 receptor ligands.
